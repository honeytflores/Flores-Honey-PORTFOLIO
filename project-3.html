PROJECT 03 · PERSONAL DATA ANALYSIS PROJECT

Automating a Personal Budget Diagnosis

Turning a hand-maintained Excel budget tracker's monthly diagnosis into a live VLOOKUP + SUMIFS pipeline — and finding two real bugs along the way.

Project type: Personal project · Tools used: Excel, VLOOKUP, SUMIFS, data cleaning

60+ MONTHLY TABS · RECONCILED TO THE PESO

PERSONAL PROJECT · FIGURES BELOW ARE ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT MY ACTUAL FINANCES

Scenario

I've kept a personal budget tracker in Excel since 2024 — a separate monthly tab logging every transaction, plus a "Diagnosis" tab that applies a needs-vs-wants framework to flag overspending. The catch: every month, I was retyping each category's total into Diagnosis by hand, reading it off the raw log myself. Could that hand-off be automated instead — and would automating it surface anything the manual process had been getting wrong?

Summary

I built a "Category Map" lookup tab pairing each Diagnosis line item to the exact category tag used in that month's raw transaction log, then rewrote the Diagnosis totals with VLOOKUP (to find the right tag) feeding into SUMIFS (to total every transaction carrying it). Updating a transaction in the raw log now flows straight through to the diagnosis — no manual re-entry. I added a reconciliation check at the bottom of the sheet comparing the automated total against the log's own running total, and it now matches exactly.

Key Challenges and Solutions

A category taxonomy that had drifted

Building the Category Map meant comparing every Diagnosis line against every category actually in use in the log — and they didn't fully agree. One Diagnosis line used different wording than its matching log category ("Dine out" vs. the log's "Eat Out"), which I mapped across explicitly. More significantly, the log had four active spending categories with no Diagnosis line at all — together, close to 30% of that month's recorded spending had nowhere to land in the diagnosis. I added the missing lines rather than forcing that spending into a category it didn't belong in.

Finding a formula bug

A separate "wishlist" tab totaled its estimated prices with =SUMIF(date_range, ">0") — summing the date column, not the price column. It went unnoticed because Excel date serial numbers and peso price estimates both land in the same numeric range, so the output still looked like a plausible total. I caught it by re-deriving the total independently and comparing. Fixed to a straight SUM over the correct column, it dropped by roughly 20%.

Verifying the automation, not just trusting it

A formula that runs without errors isn't the same as a formula that's right. I added a reconciliation row that independently sums the raw log's "actual expense" column and compares it to what the Category Map pipeline produces — if a future transaction ever gets logged under a category the map doesn't recognize, that row flags the mismatch instead of silently under-counting.

What I Learned

The most useful part of this project wasn't the formulas themselves — it was the mismatch between the two sheets that the formulas exposed. A manual process can quietly stop reflecting reality as categories evolve, and nothing flags that unless you go looking. Building the reconciliation check taught me to treat "the totals match" as something to verify with a formula, not something to assume because the sheet looks finished.

Why it Matters

This is the same discipline behind the data-cleaning exercises I've done for coursework and certification prep — reconcile totals, don't trust a formula just because it returns a number, and go looking for the mismatch instead of waiting for it to cause a problem. Here it just happened to be my own spending data instead of a practice dataset.
